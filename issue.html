🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Nubank's Mexican arm becomes a full bank, widening access for millions
Nu México, the Mexican arm of Latin America's biggest digital bank, has cleared its final regulatory hurdle with the CNBV and Ministry of Finance, and begins operating as a fully licensed commercial bank from 6 August. It can now move beyond savings accounts, personal loans and secured cards into fuller deposit-taking and banking services. With around 15 million customers already on its app — roughly one in seven Mexican adults — the shift is a marker of how digital-first providers are extending everyday banking to people traditionally underserved by branches.

Revolut's valuation jumps to $115 billion in fresh secondary sale
Revolut, the UK-founded banking app used by tens of millions for everyday spending, saving and currency exchange, has seen its valuation climb to around $115 billion through a new secondary share sale — up sharply from $75 billion a year earlier. It shows how much investor appetite remains for consumer-facing financial apps, even as the wider fintech funding market has been patchier lately. A rising valuation on paper says nothing about which specific tools improve for existing customers, but it's a useful barometer of how mainstream "everything apps" for money have become.

Pakistan's National Savings scheme finally goes mobile
Pakistan's state-run National Savings organisation, long known for requiring in-person branch visits, has launched a mobile app letting savers check balances, review transaction history, buy digital investment products and download statements from home. For a savings scheme underpinning many households' fixed-income savings, round-the-clock digital access is a small but meaningful modernisation — potentially widening participation among people, including women, who have found branch visits a barrier to managing their own money.

The week's biggest story: Big Tech's AI reckoning
Microsoft: when AI spending finally pays off
Microsoft just had its best day on the stock market since 2008. Shares jumped as much as 17% on Thursday, adding roughly $480 billion in value in a single session — reportedly the largest one-day market-cap gain any company has ever recorded. The trigger was Microsoft's fiscal fourth-quarter results: revenue of $90 billion, ahead of the $87.6 billion expected, with earnings per share of $4.74 against a forecast of $4.24. The real headline was Azure, Microsoft's cloud arm, which crossed $100 billion in annual revenue for the first time, growing 43% year-on-year, with guidance of 45% growth next quarter. For months investors had fretted that Microsoft's roughly $190 billion annual AI infrastructure spend was outpacing returns. This week's numbers, boosted partly by OpenAI's use of Azure, suggested the investment is finally converting into revenue — a reminder that markets reward spending that shows up as growth, not just spending itself.
Fortune
Meta: the other side of the AI capex story
A day earlier, Meta delivered the opposite verdict. Revenue rose 28% and beat expectations, yet shares still fell around 6–9% in after-hours trading, and net income dropped 14%. The issue was cost, not sales: Meta lifted the floor of its 2026 capital-spending guidance to $130–145 billion, and free cash flow reportedly fell by 91% to under $800 million. Combined, Microsoft and Meta's disclosed AI infrastructure spending alone now runs to roughly $170–190 billion for the year. The contrast is instructive: Microsoft sells AI capacity directly to paying enterprise customers through Azure, so the spending has a visible revenue line attached. Meta's AI investment largely serves its own apps and longer-term ambitions, with the payoff much less certain. Investors this week drew a sharp line between "AI spending we can see returns from" and "AI spending we're being asked to trust" — and priced the two very differently.

The company to follow — Microsoft
Microsoft just had one of the best days a mega-cap has ever had on Wall Street: shares jumped 15.5% on Thursday, the largest single-day market-value increase in stock-market history, after results blew past expectations. Azure cloud revenue crossed $100bn for the first time, growth accelerated to 43%, earnings per share came in at $4.74 versus $4.25 expected, and Microsoft 365 Copilot seats climbed above 30 million. Investors had been nervous about whether all that AI spending would eventually pay off — this quarter gave them a clear answer. Worth watching for how sustainable that Azure acceleration proves into next quarter, and how rivals respond.

One to be careful of — Meta Platforms
Meta's results landed very differently to Microsoft's. Shares fell around 9–10% after Q2 net income dropped 14% year-on-year, missing profit expectations, even as revenue beat forecasts on 28% growth. The company raised its 2026 capex floor to $130bn, and free cash flow compressed to just $784 million after $31bn of quarterly spending, plus one-off legal and severance charges. A useful reminder that "beating on revenue" and "beating on profit" aren't the same story, and that heavy AI investment is squeezing margins unevenly across Big Tech. Worth keeping on the watch-list as more results land and guidance gets tested — not a signal to act on either way.

Innovation in investment — T. Rowe Price's multi-token crypto ETF
T. Rowe Price has launched TKNZ, billed as the industry's first actively managed, multi-token spot crypto exchange-traded product. Rather than tracking a single coin, it holds eight digital assets — Bitcoin, Ethereum and Binance make up around 70% of the portfolio, alongside smaller positions including Stellar Lumen and a cash/stablecoin buffer — managed by veteran alternatives specialist Blue Macellari. The annual fee is 0.75%, trimmed to 0.60% under a waiver running to May 2027. A notable step for a traditionally conservative fund house moving into actively managed crypto exposure, and worth understanding as a sign of how mainstream asset managers are now packaging digital assets for everyday portfolios.

A new-CEO adventure that will make a difference — John Ghingo, Hormel Foods
Hormel Foods has named John Ghingo — currently its president, and a 25-year consumer-goods veteran with stints at Mondelēz (Oreo, Cadbury), WhiteWave and Applegate Farms — as its next chief executive, effective 26 October 2026. He succeeds interim CEO Jeff Ettinger, who steps back to the board. Ghingo rejoined Hormel in 2024 and was made president in mid-2025, so this is an internal, groomed succession rather than an outside shock appointment. Worth understanding because Hormel has been navigating a tough stretch for packaged-food volumes and pricing power — how Ghingo balances brand investment against cost pressure is one to watch through into 2027.

🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
The Fed holds firm as Wall Street's bulls push targets higher
The Federal Reserve kept interest rates on hold at 3.50–3.75% on 29 July, its fifth consecutive pause, but the 9–3 vote revealed real disagreement at the top table. Three regional presidents — Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan — dissented, arguing for a quarter-point rise instead. The committee flagged inflation still running above its 2% goal, partly blamed on energy-related supply shocks and lingering Middle East uncertainty. Markets are now pricing in the possibility of further hikes rather than cuts, a notable shift in tone. Despite that caution, equity strategists remain upbeat: Morgan Stanley, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank have set S&P 500 year-end targets of 8,000–8,300, while Evercore has gone furthest, pencilling in 9,000. The bull case rests on forecast 2026 earnings growth of 27%, led by technology and AI-related spending. The flagged risk: heavy AI capital spending eating into free cash flow, as seen recently at Meta.

DBS lets AI build the paperwork — but not yet make the calls
Singapore's DBS Bank offers a candid look at where AI in banking really stands. The bank now runs 70–80 AI agents inside its corporate lending arm, assembling credit memos for large clients, alongside tools letting staff build their own agents, and a customer-facing chatbot, "Joy", handling over 120,000 chats a month. DBS says AI unlocked roughly S$1 billion of economic value in 2025 across more than 2,000 models and 430-plus use cases. Yet DBS is deliberately withholding full autonomy from these agents. Chief Data and Transformation Officer Nimish Panchmatia put it plainly: "The speed of innovation in capability is 5x. The speed of innovation in governance is 1x." Agents optimise for efficiency, he warned, and may quietly bypass proper procedure to get there. It's a useful counterpoint to the AI-hype cycle: big US lenders report similar scale, but Jamie Dimon has cautioned against expecting fast margin gains, saying customers will be the long-term beneficiaries.

✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
On 28 December 1967, Muriel Siebert became the first woman to buy a seat on the New York Stock Exchange — after nine of the first ten men she asked to sponsor her application turned her down. The tenth said yes, and she paid $445,000 to join a room of 1,365 men, where she remained the only woman for the next decade. She went on to found her own brokerage, the first ever owned by a woman on the NYSE. A lovely reminder that persistence, not permission, is often what opens the door in finance.
