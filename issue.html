🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Monzo launches a fee-free Junior Stocks & Shares ISA
Monzo has added a Junior Stocks & Shares ISA to its banking app, charging no platform fees. A parent or guardian opens the account as Registered Contact for a child aged nought to sixteen, then chooses the investments. Up to £9,000 can go in each tax year, tax-free; the allowance resets every April and does not carry over. A shared gifting link, rolling out over the coming weeks, lets relatives and friends top up the pot directly. Seccl holds and processes the underlying cash and investments, and the account converts automatically into an adult Stocks & Shares ISA once the child turns eighteen. Monzo's wealth general manager, Jo Phillips, called it an extension of the bank's "wealth ecosystem for families." Source
OKX launches a stablecoin app for everyday saving and spending
OKX has launched OKX Money, a standalone app letting users save, spend and transfer dollar-backed stablecoins such as USDG, USDC and USDT, switching between them free of conversion charges. Spending runs through a virtual or physical card with no foreign-exchange fees — costs OKX says otherwise add two to five per cent to an overseas transaction. Deposits in more than fifty currencies convert automatically into stablecoins, and eligible USDG balances can earn up to ten per cent APY with no lock-up, plus cashback through a loyalty scheme. OKX says around seventy per cent of its target users have never used a crypto app before, so the interface hides the underlying blockchain entirely. The rollout currently covers selected emerging markets, with wider expansion planned. Source
Renters beat buyers in five UK cities, new study finds
A September 2026 study by Everest Mortgages finds renting now costs less per month than buying in several major UK cities, once maintenance and deposit costs are counted in. York tops the list: an average house price of £310,000 and a £77,500 deposit mean renters save £259 a month against buyers of detached homes, who pay £830 more monthly. London renters save nearly £280 a month despite needing no large deposit, on an average property price of £554,000; Oxford renters save over £220, Sheffield around £100 and Cardiff around £95. The study assumes a twenty-five per cent deposit and a standard five-year fixed mortgage. Everest notes under-35s are most affected, with over sixty per cent currently renting. Source

The Fed's hike goes on ice
The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate on 16 September for the first time since July 2023, taking the federal funds rate to a 3.75–4.00% range. Policymakers backed the move unanimously, driven by inflation stuck above the 2% target, fanned by the US-Iran conflict's effect on energy costs, tariff policy and AI-driven power demand. Most projections pointed to at least one further rise before year-end. Then, on 2 October, the picture wobbled: September payrolls rose by just 29,000 against forecasts above 80,000, and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%. Traders moved fast — the chance of a rate rise at the Fed's 28 October meeting, priced near 65–70% beforehand, fell to roughly 17–18% within days. The caveat: this is one soft jobs report set against a Fed that, weeks earlier, explicitly prioritised inflation over labour slack, and December hike odds remain above 65%. The debate is no longer cut-versus-hold, as in most cycles, but hold-versus-hike — an unusual dilemma for the Fed heading into year-end. Source 1 · 2 · 3
The hawkish dollar's knock-on effects
A Fed still inclined to tighten, even as growth cools, is rippling well beyond Wall Street. The 30-year US Treasury yield hit a fresh 24-year high of 5.698% on 5 October, pulling global borrowing costs up with it. Gold has held near $4,140–4,160 an ounce, just off record highs, but a dollar strengthened by the rate-hike backdrop is capping further gains. The euro has borne the brunt, sliding to a 17-month low as a French bond sell-off, triggered by scepticism over Paris's 2027 budget, pushed French-German yield spreads up by their most in a day since March 2020. Brent crude above $100 a barrel, elevated by the Iran conflict, adds to the inflation pressure that keeps the Fed hawkish in the first place. For Gulf markets, whose currencies — including the Bahraini dinar — are pegged to the dollar, this higher-for-longer US rate stance imports directly into local borrowing costs, even as elevated oil prices support government revenues: a genuinely mixed picture for GCC savers, not a one-way read. This week's four picks — educational only, never advice
The company to follow — Constellation Energy
Constellation Energy is worth watching after Google signed a fifteen-to-twenty-year, 3.6 gigawatt power-supply deal with the company on 6 October — one of the largest corporate power agreements tied to AI-driven electricity demand. Around a quarter of the supply, 890 megawatts, comes from upgrades to eleven existing nuclear reactors across Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey, backed by more than $4.3 billion of new investment; the remaining 2,700 megawatts sits within a fifteen-year supply deal. Shares rose over 13% to $304.04 the same day. The story is a clean illustration of a wider trend worth understanding: Big Tech's AI build-out is reshaping demand, and investment, in utilities and nuclear power, not only chipmakers. Worth watching how this "AI needs electricity" theme develops across the power sector. Source
One to be careful of — IG Group, and the retail trading sector
IG Group, the UK-listed online trading and derivatives platform, is worth watching for the opposite reason. On 2 October it effectively issued a profit warning, and shares fell about 25% in early trading. Quarterly revenue is tracking roughly 14% lower, core client-revenue retention dropped to 70% from a recent 80% average, and full-year revenue-growth guidance was cut to mid-single digits from a prior 10–15% forecast, with management citing "less supportive" market conditions. The read-across hit peers too: CMC Markets fell about 6% and Plus500 about 8% the same day. This is a useful case study in how quickly sentiment and guidance can turn in trading-platform businesses that depend heavily on client activity levels — worth understanding as a sector risk, not a call on any single stock. Innovation in investment — Section 351 "tax-free" ETF conversions
A structural innovation drawing fresh attention in the ETF industry is the Section 351 exchange ETF: a decades-old tax provision letting an investor contribute appreciated stock, or crypto, to a newly formed ETF without an immediate tax bill, in exchange for fund shares. Issuers have used the provision to seed new funds, letting holders of concentrated, highly appreciated positions diversify without an immediate capital-gains hit. It has become contentious enough that the US Treasury and IRS issued Notice 2026-62 on 29 September, targeting abusive variants where a fund is quickly "churned" to hand an investor a different portfolio tax-free (see Flagged — timing) — a topic still being actively discussed in trade press this week. Worth understanding as both a genuine access innovation and a flag for careful, read-the-prospectus due diligence. Source 1 · 2
A new-CEO adventure that will make a difference — Jon Newhard, Verra Mobility
Verra Mobility, the smart-mobility company behind red-light and speed-enforcement cameras, toll management and fleet services, named industry veteran Jon Newhard as its permanent President and CEO on 2 October, effective 1 November. He joins from Yunex Traffic GmbH with more than twenty years in transportation and mobility, succeeding interim CEO Jon Keyser. The backstory matters: the board removed former CEO David Roberts in June 2026, saying a change in leadership was needed to realign the company's cost structure, so Newhard's arrival closes a five-month search during a period of strategic reset. Shares rose nearly 3% in pre-market trading on the announcement. Worth watching whether a permanent, traffic-tech-focused leader can execute the cost realignment the board flagged. Source 1 · 2
🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
Market outlook: records, resilient earnings, narrowing breadth
US stocks notched multiple record closes in the first week of October, with the S&P 500 closing at 7,773.95 (+0.66%) and the Nasdaq Composite at 27,477.31 (+1.05%) on 5 October, then extending the streak the following session. The rally followed September's weak US payrolls report — just 29,000 jobs added, well below forecasts, with July and August revised down and unemployment up to 4.2% — which cut the market-implied odds of an October Fed rate rise to roughly 24%, down from about 70% a week earlier. Analysts tracked by LSEG via Reuters expect S&P 500 third-quarter earnings to grow more than 24–30% year-on-year, an eighth straight quarter of double-digit growth, led by AI-exposed names: Nvidia's third-quarter earnings are forecast to rise 90%. Big US banks open the reporting season on 13 October, seen as the next test for the rally. B. Riley strategist Art Hogan flagged falling oil prices, with Brent near $100, as a tailwind. Risks flagged include narrow market breadth, 10-year Treasury yields near 24-year highs around 5.3%, and lingering Middle East-linked geopolitical risk. Source 1 · 2 · 3
How banks are evolving: Barclays scales Claude, JPMorgan tops the AI rankings
Barclays announced on 2 October that it is expanding its use of Anthropic's Claude across the bank to modernise legacy systems and lift developer output, targeting 50% of developers using Claude Code by the end of 2026 and a majority of software engineers by 2027. Its Colleague Knowledge Assistant, live since 2025, is now used by over 16,000 staff supporting more than 20 million UK retail customers, logging over one million searches; in Global Markets, Claude processes roughly 120,000 client emails daily, triaging and routing enquiries before human review. Deputy Co-Chief Operating Officer Anne Marie Darling said the measure of the technology is "the impact it has on customers, clients, and colleagues." Separately, data-intelligence firm Evident's 2026 AI Index, published 6 October and scoring fifty global banks, ranked JPMorgan Chase first for a fifth consecutive year, citing an in-house large-language-model suite and estimated annual AI-driven benefits of about $2 billion, up from $1–1.5 billion previously. Capital One and RBC ranked second and third; UBS was the only European bank in the top ten. Bank AI capability advanced nearly three times faster this year than the prior three-year average, Evident found. Source 1 · 2
✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
Paper money may have started life as… tree bark
In Tang Dynasty China's Sichuan province, between the seventh and tenth centuries, merchant guilds grew tired of lugging heavy strings of coins, so they began printing "flying checks" on mulberry tree bark instead — lightweight, portable promises of value considered an early ancestor of the modern banknote. Centuries before contactless payments, someone solved "my purse is too heavy" with bark from a tree. Next time your wallet feels weighty, spare a thought for the Sichuan traders who got there first, with a rustle rather than a jingle. Source
