🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Klarna brings instalment payments to the beauty chair
Klarna has partnered with booking platform Fresha to let clients of beauty and wellness businesses spread the cost of appointments. Announced on 2 November, the buy-now-pay-later option sits inside Fresha's payment tools, staying linked to bookings, payments and client records. It targets higher-value treatments — aesthetics, laser work, advanced skincare and premium hair services — where upfront costs can run into hundreds of pounds. Fresha, which serves more than 140,000 partner businesses across over 120 countries and processes over $1.4bn in monthly transaction value, already runs its own lending arm, Fresha Capital, which has issued more than $20m in loans, with over 80% of borrowers returning for further financing. Worth knowing about if instalment payments feature in your own spending. PYMNTS, 2 Nov 2026 · DesignRush
The 30-7-1 rule for a debt-free Black Friday
With 44% of US shoppers expecting to go into holiday debt this year, and Adobe forecasting more than $21bn in buy-now-pay-later-funded holiday spending, budgeting app PocketGuard is promoting a simple method: the 30-7-1 rule. Thirty days before big sales, build a wishlist. Seven days before, set a hard price ceiling for each item. One day before, do a final review and strip out anything non-essential. PocketGuard's own tools track disposable income after bills and compare spend by merchant or category. The US National Retail Federation projects $1.014 trillion in November–December retail sales this year, up 4.2%, much of it inflation-driven. A useful, no-cost framework for anyone shopping this season. FF News, 2 Nov 2026
Revolut takes on the high-street banks with personal loans
Revolut has launched unsecured personal loans in Britain, chief executive Francesca Carlesi confirmed on 27 October — a direct challenge to the big banks' lending dominance (see ⚑ Flagged). Loans range from £2,000 to £25,000, with interest rates between 6.9% and 29.3%. The rollout starts with a small group of customers before expanding to Revolut's 14 million UK users over the coming months. Carlesi said a Revolut credit card is coming soon, with mortgages pencilled in as a longer-term goal. Revolut now serves more than 80 million customers worldwide and carries a private valuation of $115bn. Worth watching as a sign of how far fintechs are moving into mainstream banking. Cyprus Mail via Reuters, 27 Oct 2026

What happened: Wall Street's worst day in over a year, two days after a political earthquake
Wall Street suffered its worst one-day sell-off since April 2025 on Thursday 5 November, two days after Democrats secured control of both the House and Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections. The S&P 500 fell 4.4%, the Nasdaq slid sharply to a seven-month low (see ⚑ Flagged), and the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1,833 points, or 3.44%, to close at 51,523. All eleven S&P 500 sectors finished lower. Chipmakers took the heaviest losses: Micron fell 17.5%, AMD 17.3%, storage maker SanDisk 22.3% and Broadcom 10.4%, with Nvidia down around 9.2%. The rout followed weeks of strategist warnings that a Democratic sweep would intensify congressional scrutiny of artificial-intelligence infrastructure and data-centre construction. By Friday 6 November, US stock futures had stabilised and begun recovering some of the lost ground. Trading Economics · Al Jazeera
Why it matters: politics is now repricing the AI trade
The sell-off crystallises a risk strategists have flagged for months: that political change in Washington could directly reprice the artificial-intelligence trade that has powered much of this year's bull market. With Democrats expected to intensify scrutiny of data-centre energy use, permitting and AI company practices, investors are now pricing in slower, costlier AI build-outs rather than unchecked capital spending. A Reuters/Ipsos poll reported on 22 October found 69% of Democratic voters back limits on data-centre construction, against 43% of Republicans. Because semiconductor and hyperscaler stocks have driven a disproportionate share of 2026's index gains, their reversal threatens to drag broader benchmarks down even as the underlying economy keeps expanding. The episode lands weeks after the Federal Reserve held rates steady while flagging persistent inflation, and Fed chair Jerome Powell has separately described a December rate cut as "not a foregone conclusion" (see ⚑ Flagged). Equities now face a tighter policy backdrop and a fresh political one at the same time. Kitco · Federal Reserve

The company to follow
AMD's chips keep beating the doubters
AMD's shares jumped roughly 9% in after-hours trading on 3 November after the chipmaker reported record third-quarter revenue of $13.38bn, up 45% year-on-year and ahead of the roughly $13bn Wall Street expected. The standout line was data-centre revenue — the segment housing AMD's AI accelerators — which rose to $8.55bn, nearly double the prior year and above forecasts. Gaming revenue also beat expectations, at $773m against $655m expected, and adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.09. The result landed in a week when several AI-linked names beat estimates yet still saw shares fall, making AMD's clean beat-and-rally worth understanding as a reference point for how selective the market currently is about rewarding AI spending. Digg, 3 Nov 2026
One to be careful of
Oracle leans harder on debt to fund its AI build-out
Oracle shares fell roughly 8–9% on 2 November after the company launched a $20bn stock-and-bond offering — $10bn of new shares priced at an 11.2% discount, plus $10bn of bonds carrying coupons of 7% to 8.65% — to help fund its AI and cloud infrastructure expansion. The raise underlines the scale of debt Oracle is taking on: it already carried $125.3bn in borrowings as of 31 August, against free cash flow that was negative $23.7bn in fiscal 2026. Moody's keeps a negative outlook on the credit. Oracle reaffirmed ambitious growth targets through 2030, and shares partly rebounded later in the week. A useful case study in how this AI capex cycle is increasingly funded by debt and dilution rather than cash flow alone. IBTimes UK · Finviz

🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
Central banks turn cautious just as the AI rally cracks
Three of the world's most-watched central banks struck a more guarded tone this week, just as this year's AI-driven stock rally showed its first real wobble. On 2 November, European Central Bank vice-president Boris Vujcic named stretched valuations in AI-linked stocks, stress in government bond markets, and potential losses at non-bank lenders as the three biggest current threats to financial stability, ahead of the ECB's Financial Stability Review due on 18 November. He noted the Nasdaq had fallen nearly 8% from its all-time high following lukewarm reactions to results from Alphabet, Intel and Amazon, adding that "everybody's exposed to that... including Europe." In the US, Federal Reserve chair Jerome Powell has described a December rate cut as "not a foregone conclusion" (see ⚑ Flagged), unsettling traders who had leaned towards further easing. In the UK, Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey said on 6 November that rates are "likely to continue on a gradual downward path", having held the Bank Rate at 4%. Analysts note the S&P 500's forward price-to-earnings ratio has climbed above 23 times — its highest since the dot-com era — leaving little room for disappointment. The Edge Malaysia · The Standard HK · FXStreet
NatWest ties fresh job cuts directly to its AI rollout
NatWest has become the latest UK high-street bank to link job losses explicitly to artificial intelligence. Third-quarter results published on 30 October showed around 1,000 full-time roles cut during the quarter, taking 2026's total to 1,400. Operating pre-tax profit rose 2% year-on-year to £2.2bn, while cost savings of roughly £420m helped push the bank's cost-to-income ratio down to 45.7% for the year to date. Chief executive Paul Thwaite said AI is now "pervasive across the organisation," with trials under way for an AI spending-insights tool and a fraud-detection "agent" for credit-card customers; some staff are moving into newly created AI-focused roles even as others are restructured out. He declined to rule out further cuts. The pattern echoes HSBC's third-quarter results, where net profit rose 45.5% to $7bn even as the bank pressed ahead with AI-linked restructuring in its UK wealth-management arm (see ⚑ Flagged). Together, the results suggest UK banks are using strong earnings to fund faster automation of back-office and advisory work, rather than slowing the pace of change. ITV News · Malay Mail
✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
Monopoly money hid real cash to help prisoners of war escape
During the Second World War, British board-game maker John Waddington Ltd, then the UK licensee for Monopoly, secretly produced doctored sets for Allied prisoners of war in German camps. Hidden inside the box and beneath the stacks of play money, sets smuggled in silk escape maps, tiny compasses, metal files — and genuine German, Italian and French banknotes tucked under the fake Monopoly notes. The sets were disguised as ordinary charity-donated games, routed via fictitious aid organisations working with British intelligence unit MI9, so German guards had no idea "Monopoly night" was actually an escape kit. A reminder that money, real or fake, has always found creative hiding places. Snopes · CNN / Mental Floss
