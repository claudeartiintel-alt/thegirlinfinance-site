🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
NatWest launches a savings account built for people who like being in control
NatWest has introduced First Active, a digital savings account offering 4.5% AER for the first year (a 1.5% bonus on a 3% variable rate, reverting to 3% after 12 months). It opens online with £1 and allows instant access up to £250,000 per deposit. NatWest's own research found 53% of savers rank "instant access" as their top priority — ahead of the headline rate itself. Worth knowing if you're weighing up easy-access accounts for a first emergency fund: flexibility can matter as much as chasing the top number. Comparing rates and reversion terms before committing remains sensible.

Revolut secures a full French banking licence
Revolut has been granted a full banking licence by French and European regulators — a milestone for the fintech, which now serves around 30 million customers across Western Europe. Rollout begins in France before extending to Germany, Ireland, Italy, Portugal and Spain, run alongside its existing Lithuanian licence under a "dual-hub" model. Licensing news like this is worth knowing because it often precedes more localised products and clearer regulatory protections in each market — a useful signal that the challenger-bank era is entering a more established phase, if you're deciding how much everyday money to trust to newer digital banks.

£11 million in Tesco Clubcard vouchers about to expire
MoneySavingExpert reports roughly £11 million worth of Tesco Clubcard vouchers expire at 11.59pm on Monday 31 August 2026. Why a tanker strike in the Gulf matters to your portfolio
This week brought the sharpest reminder yet that the US-Iran war, now in its sixth month, remains the single biggest swing factor for global markets. On 11–12 August, the US Navy fired on the tanker Vela Nova for breaching Washington's blockade on Iranian ports, while Houthi forces struck a commercial vessel near the Bab al-Mandab Strait, killing six sailors. Brent crude rose to $89.81 a barrel, up around 7% in five days and roughly 40% since the start of the year. Shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — which normally carries a fifth of the world's oil and gas — has collapsed from an average of eleven vessels a day to as few as six. Diplomacy stalled further after President Trump demanded compensation from Iran spanning fifty years of grievances as a condition for reopening the strait. US officials now reportedly expect these disruptions to persist into 2027, meaning higher, stickier energy costs look set to remain a macro theme for some time yet.

The odd calm — why Wall Street keeps hitting records anyway
You might expect a war disrupting a fifth of the world's oil supply to hammer stock markets. Instead, Wall Street has been remarkably resilient. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,798.99 on 13 August — its 27th record close of the year — after July's Consumer Price Index came in at 3.4% annually and producer prices stayed flat against forecasts. That combination pushed traders to price a 63% chance the Federal Reserve holds rates steady in September, a sharp reversal from expectations of another hike just weeks earlier. London's FTSE 100 has told a similar story, recovering to a record above 10,900 in late July as higher oil prices lifted energy giants BP and Shell. The picture is more mixed closer to the conflict: Gulf markets, including Bahrain, dipped on 11 August as hopes of a US-Iran deal faded. Worth watching how global and regional indices keep telling different stories.

THE FOUR PICKS
The company to follow
Axon Enterprise
Axon, best known for Tasers and police body cameras, had a week worth noticing. Its Q2 results beat forecasts on both revenue ($904.4m, versus $876.5m expected) and adjusted earnings ($1.88 a share). What stood out was what's driving growth: subscription revenue from its "Platform Solutions" arm jumped 123%, its AI-powered offering grew nearly 700%, and counter-drone technology crossed $100m in revenue for the first time. Management raised full-year revenue growth guidance to 32–34%. It's a useful example of a "hardware" company quietly becoming a software and AI subscription business — worth understanding as a pattern playing out across many older industrial names right now.

One to be careful of
HubSpot
HubSpot's week is a lesson in why headline numbers don't tell the whole story. The marketing-software firm technically beat expectations on both earnings ($3.26 versus $3.02 forecast) and revenue — yet shares initially fell around 20% after hours before partly recovering, because full-year guidance came in below what analysts wanted. Management flagged macro headwinds, longer sales cycles and AI-related pricing changes as reasons growth is taking longer to show up. Several banks cut their price targets even after the beat. Nothing here points to trouble at the company itself — it also announced a $1bn share buyback — but it's a good moment to note how guidance, not just past results, moves share prices, and worth watching how the next few quarters play out.

Innovation in investment
BlackRock's tokenised money-market funds
Earlier this month BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager — introduced two tokenised cash products: "OnChain" shares of its Select Treasury Based Liquidity Fund, and a Daily Reinvestment Stablecoin Reserve Vehicle. In plain English, these let investors hold a traditional, low-risk money-market fund but represented and settled using blockchain technology rather than the usual paperwork-heavy fund infrastructure. It follows BlackRock's earlier tokenised fund, BUIDL, launched in 2024, and reflects a broader race among major banks to bring traditional assets onto blockchain rails. Worth understanding, even if you never touch crypto directly, as this kind of "plumbing" change could quietly reshape how ordinary funds are bought, held and settled in future.

A new-CEO adventure that will make a difference
Lee McIntire at Fermi
Fermi, the AI-and-nuclear-power infrastructure company, named Lee McIntire as its new chief executive, effective 11 August 2026 — just days ago. McIntire is no newcomer to big builds: over a four-decade career he has held senior roles at Bechtel, led CH2M Hill, run TerraPower (the nuclear firm backed by Bill Gates), and worked on the Panama Canal expansion. He had already served as an independent Fermi board director since September 2025. The appointment matters because Fermi is moving from planning into construction of "Project Matador", a vast 17-gigawatt private electric grid designed to power AI data centres. Worth watching as a signal of how seriously energy-hungry AI infrastructure is now being taken by seasoned industrial leaders, not just tech executives.

🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
What Wall Street is forecasting right now — and why the picture is split
This week brought two very different messages from the banks that move markets. On 10 August, JPMorgan raised its year-end S&P 500 target to 8,000 (from 7,800), pointing to stronger-than-expected corporate earnings and evidence AI spending is finally showing up in results — by then 87% of S&P 500 firms had reported Q2 earnings, broadly beating forecasts. But the mood isn't uniformly upbeat. Two days earlier, Bank of America flagged a key sentiment gauge at its highest since 2021 and urged clients toward a "summer retreat" into defensive sectors. Goldman Sachs noted nearly half of Q2's headline earnings growth came from book-value revaluation rather than operating profit, with AI infrastructure stocks alone driving a third of growth. Société Générale separately warned inflation expectations may be sitting too low given tariffs, capex and oil volatility. Forecasts for the S&P 500 now range from a cautious 7,100 to a bullish 8,100 — genuine disagreement about how long investors will keep paying premium prices for AI-driven profits.

How banks are evolving — AI moves from pilot projects to daily banking
The clearest trend in banking this year is AI moving out of the lab and into everyday operations. During Q2 2026 earnings calls, Bank of America said over 200,000 staff now use its AI tools, generating more than 400,000 prompts a day across 300 approved use cases. Citigroup said "nearly nine out of ten" of its people now use the bank's AI tools, while JPMorgan runs close to 1,000 live AI use cases spanning fraud detection and risk management — though its CEO cautioned that customers, not shareholders, may be the biggest near-term beneficiaries. Wells Fargo and BNY Mellon are rolling out similar tools for advisers and staff. The technology is also getting more capable: on 12 August, customer-engagement firm Glia launched 25 AI agents built for banks and credit unions, able to freeze lost cards, process payments and open disputes within a live conversation. A sign that banks are shifting from AI that merely chats to AI that actually executes transactions on a customer's behalf.

✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
The gravity genius who moonlit as a fraud detective
Sir Isaac Newton — yes, the gravity one — had a secret second career as a currency-crime detective. In 1696 he became Warden of the Royal Mint, tasked with fixing a Britain where roughly one in ten coins in circulation was a fake. Rather than stay in his laboratory, Newton went undercover in disguise, trawling London's grubbiest taverns for intelligence and personally cross-examining more than 100 suspects. He built the case that sent his arch-rival, master forger William Chaloner, to the gallows at Tyburn in 1699. Proof that the smartest man in history also moonlighted as a Georgian fraud squad.
