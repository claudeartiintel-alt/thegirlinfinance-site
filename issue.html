🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
A women-first investing app launches in India
SheFin, a new investment app aimed specifically at women, has launched on the App Store and Google Play this month, built by Mumbai-based wealth firm InvesTek. Rather than offering generic products, it frames investing around women's life stages — early saving, marriage, career breaks and retirement — pairing goal-based planning with financial education and guidance. The launch leans on AMFI data showing women now make up over a quarter of India's individual mutual fund investors, with their share of assets rising year on year. It is a reminder that women-focused investing apps are a growing category worldwide, not a niche — worth knowing about even outside India, as similar tools may well follow elsewhere. (see Flagged)

Cash ISA allowance set to shrink — what's coming
Ahead of the UK's Autumn Budget on 28 October, reporting this week set out previously announced changes due from the 2027/28 tax year. Under-65s' cash ISA allowance is expected to fall from £20,000 to £12,000, with transfers from stocks and shares ISAs into cash ISAs restricted, and a 22% charge proposed on interest earned on uninvested cash held within non-cash ISAs. A new First Time Buyer ISA is pencilled in to eventually replace the Lifetime ISA, probably from 2028. None of this is confirmed until the Budget itself (see Flagged), but it is worth knowing now: anyone who relies heavily on cash ISAs may want to watch the announcement closely and think ahead about how savings are split between cash and investments.

Fixed savings rates pass 5% for the first time since 2023
New data this week from Moneyfacts shows the best one- to five-year fixed savings rates have climbed above 5% AER, the highest since December 2023. The average one-year fixed rate now stands at 4.41%, up for a seventh consecutive month, while some fixed cash ISAs pay over 5% too — useful to know if tax-free returns matter. Cash ISA numbers have also hit a record high, giving savers more choice. It is a good moment for anyone with a fixed bond maturing soon, or money sitting in a low-paying easy-access account, to compare what is on offer rather than letting a provider roll it over automatically. As ever, rates move quickly, so check current figures before deciding anything.

Why government borrowing costs just hit a 24-year high
Global bond markets delivered the sharpest macro signal of the week: the US 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.36% on Wednesday 8 October, its highest level since 2002, before easing to 5.24% by Friday. The move followed the Federal Reserve's 16 September decision to raise its benchmark rate by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since 2023, after a unanimous vote. Minutes released this week showed most policymakers expect at least one further rise before year-end, citing a stubborn prices component in September's services survey. A $39bn 10-year note auction tested investor appetite directly into this backdrop. The dynamic was not confined to America: French 10-year yields breached 5% on 1 October, their highest since 2002, as investors priced in France's 119%-of-GDP debt load and a minority government's uncertain path through a €54bn budget-consolidation bill. The French-German spread widened to a 14-year high. Together, the two moves illustrate a theme running through this week's markets: developed-economy governments are now borrowing at levels unseen in a generation. Stocks hit records anyway, carried by bank earnings
Despite surging borrowing costs, US equities kept climbing. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,818.93 on Tuesday 6 October and finished the week at 7,811.54, up 1.2%; the Dow reached 51,654.95. The catalyst was the opening of third-quarter bank earnings season. Goldman Sachs reported diluted EPS of $17.01 against a $13.65 consensus and revenue of $18.68bn versus $17.03bn expected, sending shares higher. JPMorgan Chase was roughly flat on its own results. Wells Fargo was the exception, falling around 3% after Q3 revenue of $21.87bn missed expectations. Elsewhere, Schneider Electric's agreed $22.6bn, $205-a-share takeover of industrial-software group PTC sent PTC shares up roughly 34%, while OpenAI's disclosure of about $50bn in annualised revenue — below the $68–70bn figure that had circulated the market — contributed to a 3% fall in Nvidia and a 3.4% drop in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index. The split outcome underlines how this week's market strength rested on individual corporate results and deal activity rather than a broad reading of interest-rate policy. The company to follow — Goldman Sachs
Goldman Sachs' Q3 2026 results, released this week, are worth understanding as a signal of where Wall Street momentum currently sits. Diluted EPS of $17.01 blew past the $13.65 consensus, and revenue of $18.68bn beat the $17.03bn estimate. The standout was equities trading, up 72% year-on-year to $6.44bn on strong prime brokerage and derivatives activity, alongside a record $1.65bn in advisory fees and assets under supervision hitting an all-time high of $4.1 trillion. Not everything was clean — the CET1 capital ratio fell to 12.4% from 14.3%, and the investment-banking fee backlog declined versus Q2 — but the headline strength in trading and wealth management makes Goldman a useful bellwether for how capital-markets activity is holding up heading into year-end. Worth watching how durable the equities-trading surge proves next quarter.

One to be careful of — AI/semiconductor stocks
A real, sourced headwind hit chipmakers this week. On 8 October, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI's annualised revenue run-rate is tracking near $50bn — roughly $20bn below the figure markets had been pricing in. Because OpenAI's growth underpins expectations for data-centre buildout (and therefore chip demand), the news triggered a broad sell-off: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell around 3.4%, with Intel down 5.3%, Micron down nearly 4.8%, Broadcom down 4.3% and Nvidia down almost 3%. Even Broadcom's and Intel's own chief executives flagged capital access and financing costs as potential constraints on AI infrastructure spending, despite describing near-term demand as still strong. This is a sector worth watching closely for signs of whether the AI capex story is cooling or just pausing — not a signal to act on, but a genuine data point on sentiment fragility in a crowded trade.

Innovation in investment — DTCC's tokenisation service goes live
The Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC) — the backbone that clears and settles most US securities trades — is rolling out a genuinely new piece of market infrastructure this month: a service that tokenises assets held at the Depository Trust Company (stocks, ETFs, Treasuries) on blockchain rails, confirmed for commercial launch in October 2026. It builds on a December 2025 SEC No-Action Letter and limited live-production trades that began in July 2026. More than 50 institutions are involved, including BlackRock, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Nasdaq and NYSE. It is worth understanding because it could eventually mean faster settlement, fractional ownership and lower transaction costs for ordinary index-fund and ETF holdings — a structural shift in how shares you already own get recorded and moved, not a new product to buy.

A new-CEO adventure that will make a difference — John Ternus at Apple
John Ternus, Apple's longtime hardware engineering chief, became chief executive on 1 September 2026, succeeding Tim Cook (who moved to executive chairman) after 15 years at the helm. It is worth understanding what is at stake: Ternus inherits a company whose market value grew from around $350bn to over $4 trillion under Cook, but also a strategy that has so far avoided huge AI data-centre spending in favour of betting on device quality and Apple's role as the gateway to AI services. Analysts note Apple's AI features have lagged and Siri still leans on Google, so Ternus's early choices on AI platform strategy — alongside this year's iPhone lineup and newer products such as the budget MacBook Neo — are being watched as the first real test of post-Cook Apple.

🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
Markets heading into year-end: cautious optimism, with fault lines showing
Major US banks' third-quarter results, released this week, offered the clearest read yet on where markets stand. JPMorgan Chase reported profit up 6% year-on-year to $15.3bn (shy of forecasts, with expenses up 12% and $658m in securities losses), while Goldman Sachs posted $5.1bn, up 33% annually on a 72% jump in equities trading revenue. Citigroup's profit rose 40% and Wells Fargo's 6.5%. Goldman chief executive David Solomon described conditions as a "very strong technology super cycle," expecting AI-driven capital spending — he cited estimates exceeding $10 trillion in US data-centre buildout through 2032 — to continue, with Goldman positioning itself to finance much of it. JPMorgan's CFO Jeremy Barnum confirmed capital deployment behind AI ventures remains strong. Both banks called consumer spending healthy, though Barnum flagged that further rate rises could lift unemployment and strain consumer credit. Separately, a 7 October roundup of bank commentary found JPMorgan "constructive" on equities with the US as its core overweight, and Deutsche Bank noting resilient Australian households (see Flagged). Technically, the S&P 500 has traded in a roughly 7,620–7,800 range since August, with October's rotation out of semiconductors prompting analysts to counsel selectivity rather than broad de-risking. How banks are evolving: Wall Street repositions around financing the AI build-out
This week's third-quarter earnings season revealed a clear strategic shift: major banks are recasting themselves as financiers of the AI infrastructure boom, not just beneficiaries of trading volatility around it. Goldman Sachs' David Solomon said the bank expects to "play a major role" in funding the data-centre and chip buildout, which some studies estimate will cost more than $10 trillion by 2032. This financing push is already visible in results — Goldman's equities trading revenue jumped 72% year-on-year, and investment banking fees rose, partly on deal activity tied to AI infrastructure. JPMorgan's CFO Jeremy Barnum said capital spending commitments behind AI ventures remain strong and undiminished. Bankers at both firms acknowledged public unease — around rogue AI agents, job displacement, and the energy and water demands of data centres — but said none of this has materially slowed deal flow or capital deployment so far. The pattern marks a notable evolution from banks treating AI mainly as an internal efficiency tool toward treating it as a primary balance-sheet growth engine, underwriting bonds, loans and equity raises for hyperscalers and data-centre operators. Whether this concentration of bank revenue in one theme proves durable will depend on AI capital expenditure continuing at its current pace. This is educational commentary, not financial advice.

✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
The money too big to steal
On the Micronesian island of Yap, "money" could be a limestone disc the size of a millstone — some rai stones measure 3.6 metres across and weigh 4,000 kilograms. But the best bit is what happened when one sank. A giant rai being ferried by canoe slipped overboard and dropped to the seabed, never to be seen again. Did that ruin its owner? Not a bit — the whole village simply agreed the stone was still down there, so it carried on being traded, bought and inherited exactly like any coin you could hold. Pure value by consensus, no vault required.
