🌱  Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Revolut confirms breach after fraudsters spoof a government email domain
Revolut has confirmed a data breach after fraudsters used a legitimate government agency's email domain to submit fake requests for customer information. The bank says a limited number of customers were affected; exposed data included identity documents, verification selfies, addresses and transaction histories. Revolut says customer funds and its own systems were untouched, and it has alerted regulators, law enforcement and the impersonated agency. A useful reminder for anyone banking digitally: even major institutions can be fooled by convincing impersonation. Good habits worth keeping — verify any unexpected request about your account through your banking app rather than a link or caller, and check statements regularly for anything unfamiliar. X lets US users trade shares and crypto without leaving the app
Social platform X has launched "Cashtags" — clickable ticker symbols such as $AAPL that open a live price chart with a "Trade" button linking to partner brokerages including Interactive Brokers, Moomoo, Coinbase, Kraken and Gemini. US users can browse market chatter and pricing side by side, then complete any transaction through their own brokerage login. It is a striking sign of how investing conversation and execution are merging on everyday social platforms — handy for quick research, but worth treating with care. Frictionless access from a scrolling feed can make it easier to act on a passing headline than on a considered plan, so pausing before following the crowd is worth remembering. TechCrunch ↗
Bahrain's Tarabut raises $50m to expand open banking across the Gulf
Bahrain-headquartered open banking platform Tarabut has secured $50 million from a group of Gulf investors, including Riyad Bank, GIB Saudi Arabia, X-Tech Fund and Kanoo Ventures, to expand its "embedded finance" infrastructure across Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Bahrain. Tarabut connects banks and lenders via secure APIs, letting approved apps assess creditworthiness from real financial behaviour rather than paperwork alone, which can speed up financing decisions for individuals and small businesses. Founded in 2017, the firm says it has now processed more than five billion API calls regionally — a good example of how the financial "plumbing" behind everyday apps keeps evolving, with Gulf investors backing more connected, data-driven services. Why the Fed just raised rates — and why that's unusual
The US Federal Reserve did something markets were not fully braced for this week: it raised interest rates rather than cutting them. On 16 September, the Fed lifted its benchmark rate by a quarter point to a range of 3.75%–4.00%, the first increase since 2023. Chair Kevin Warsh was blunt about the reasoning, saying plainly that inflation is too high and has been for too long. Sixteen of the eighteen Fed policymakers signalled they expect at least one further hike before year-end.

For anyone building money confidence, here is why it matters. Central banks raise rates when they are worried prices are rising too fast — a hike makes borrowing (mortgages, credit cards, loans) more expensive, which is meant to cool spending and bring inflation back down. It also tends to make savings accounts and some bonds pay a little more. Markets wobbled initially, then recovered the next day as bond yields eased. It is a reminder that rates do not simply drift downward over time — they respond to real pressures, which this week were unusually intense. CNBC ↗
The oil shock behind the world's inflation problem
Behind this week's central bank moves sits a geopolitical story. An escalating conflict has been disrupting some of the world's busiest oil shipping lanes: on 10 September, Houthi forces seized the Red Sea port city of Mokha, adding pressure to routes through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb strait — corridors that normally carry around a fifth of the world's crude oil and gas. Brent crude touched $109 a barrel mid-week before settling near $104.60, still its highest sustained level in months.

For everyday finances, this matters because oil is a cost built into almost everything — fuel, food transport, manufacturing, heating. When oil prices jump because of war or shipping disruption, inflation tends to follow, which is exactly the pressure that pushed the Fed to hike and put the Bank of England on watch, with Governor Andrew Bailey warning inflation could approach 4% by early 2027. It is a useful, real-time example of how geopolitics, energy markets and the interest rate on your own savings account are all quietly connected. The National ↗ · CP24 ↗

The company to follow
CrowdStrike Holdings
CrowdStrike is worth watching after its shares jumped around 12–15% this week, one of the sharpest single-week moves among large-cap tech names. The unusual catalyst: weekend essays from Anthropic's Dario Amodei and OpenAI's Sam Altman urging a slower pace of AI development were read by markets as a signal that the threat environment — and enterprise security budgets — will only grow. CrowdStrike's chief executive, George Kurtz, reinforced that reading, arguing frontier AI labs will keep advancing regardless, making cyber-defence spend essential. Fresh product launches at the company's Fal.Con conference and analyst upgrades added to the move. Worth understanding: pure-play security names outran the broader cybersecurity sector, suggesting investors were being selective about which companies stand to benefit most. Yahoo Finance ↗
One to be careful of
Micron Technology
Micron is worth approaching with care this week. Shares fell more than 5% on 14 September to $924.90 — 26% below their 52-week high — as the same AI-slowdown commentary that lifted cybersecurity stocks weighed on chipmakers instead. The worry: if AI development genuinely paces down, demand for the memory chips that power AI data centres could soften. Rivals SK Hynix, SanDisk and Western Digital all fell too, and South Korea's Kospi dropped 3% in sympathy. Layered on top are broader headwinds — Treasury yields near 5%, oil above $100 a barrel, possible new US semiconductor tariffs, and an active patent lawsuit from Netlist over DDR5 memory. Micron reports earnings on 30 September, and some investors are visibly trimming positions beforehand. Worth understanding, not acting on: analysts remain split, with the underlying AI memory-demand story still seen by some as intact. Blockonomi ↗
Innovation in investment
PWRX — the first ETF on the Texas Stock Exchange
A genuinely new product worth understanding: on 17 September, asset manager Westwood launched the Westwood Salient Enhanced Power & Infrastructure ETF (ticker PWRX) — the first fund ever to list on the newly opened Texas Stock Exchange, a rival venue to the NYSE and Nasdaq. PWRX is actively managed, investing across traditional energy and utilities, grid infrastructure, data-centre infrastructure and next-generation energy technologies — essentially the companies benefiting from AI-driven electricity demand. It pairs equity holdings with a covered-call options overlay designed to generate monthly income alongside potential growth. Worth understanding: this is as much a story about market infrastructure — a brand-new exchange finding its feet — as it is about the fund itself, and both are useful examples of how investing keeps evolving. John Ternus, Apple

🔮  Investment: the bigger picture
Market predictions: has the AI-driven rally peaked?
A fresh wave of forecasts this week split opinion on where markets go next. Analysts at Capital Economics, in commentary reported by Fortune, expect the S&P 500 to climb roughly 7.7% by the end of 2026 to around 8,250 points — then warn of a sharp 21% reversal to about 6,500 by the end of 2027. Their reasoning: several classic pre-peak signals are flashing together, including valuations approaching dot-com-era extremes, unsustainable earnings growth, heavy equity issuance, and expectations that AI "hyperscaler" companies could post negative free cash flow in 2027.

A second flashpoint is bond yields. Ruchir Sharma of Rockefeller International has pointed to the US 10-year Treasury yield — which touched 4.97% in mid-September — as a line in the sand: a decisive break above 5% could make it harder to finance the vast capital spending behind the AI boom. Veteran strategist Ed Yardeni recently trimmed his own bullish-scenario odds from 80% to 70%. The takeaway: forecasts diverge sharply, and even seasoned analysts are hedging. This is educational commentary, not a signal to act on. Fortune ↗
How banks are evolving: regulators build an AI rulebook
Banks have spent the past year racing to deploy AI — but this week brought a reminder that oversight is catching up fast. On 16 September, the Conference of State Bank Supervisors, representing regulators across all 50 US states, DC and four territories, published its new AI Supervisory Framework: a toolkit examiners will use to scrutinise how state-chartered banks and state-licensed nonbank financial firms are using artificial intelligence.

The framework fills a gap left by federal regulators. Guidance issued in April by the Federal Reserve, the OCC and the FDIC explicitly excluded generative and agentic AI, calling the technologies novel and rapidly evolving, and applied mainly to larger institutions. The new state framework has no such size cut-off — it covers banks of all sizes, meaning roughly 3,355 institutions, about 79% of all US banks, could face new AI-related questions at their next routine exam. Examiners will ask eight foundational questions covering where AI is deployed, whether it touches customers, and how sensitive data is handled. The story illustrates a broader shift: as banks lean further into AI, regulators are moving from watching to actively testing how it is controlled. American Banker ↗
✨  Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
Here's a proper stranger-than-fiction one. For roughly 700 years, England's Exchequer did not keep its accounts on paper — it kept them on sticks. Notched "tally sticks" recorded who owed the Crown what, then were split lengthwise, one half to the Treasury and one half to the taxpayer, so neither side could quietly rewrite history. Wonderfully secure, until 1834, when officials decided the tidiest way to dispose of centuries of retired tally sticks was to burn them in the underfloor stoves beneath the House of Lords. The fire got away from them entirely and took the whole Palace of Westminster down with it. Moral of the story: even the government's bookkeeping has, at least once, gone up in flames.
