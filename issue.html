🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Starling Bank starts rolling out weekly AI “smart tools” for budgeting and saving
Starling Bank has begun releasing a steady stream of AI-powered “smart tools” inside its banking app, with new ones promised every week for the rest of 2026. Early additions include a Student Budget Planner that maps income against living costs, a Spending Quiz testing how well you really know your own habits, and a forthcoming Rainy Day Saver that works out a realistic saving amount and moves it automatically. Built into the bank’s AI assistant, the tools are being crowdsourced from customer suggestions rather than designed top-down. Worth knowing for anyone building their first proper budget: money tools are shifting from static spreadsheets towards small, automated nudges that do the categorising and saving for you. Bahrain’s BenefitPay marks its 100th customer-driven upgrade
Bahrain’s national digital wallet, BenefitPay, has just rolled out its 100th customer-requested upgrade — a homepage shortcut to your personal QR code for faster transfers and payments. Built with fintech partner FOO, the wallet now serves over 1.3 million active subscribers, logging 1.7 million app visits a day and more than 1.9 billion transactions to date across bill payments, remittances and contactless spending. It is a marker of how far Gulf digital payments infrastructure has travelled: a basic e-wallet has grown into an everyday money-management hub spanning more than twenty services. Worth knowing if you rely on a single app for day-to-day spending, saving and sending money — the region’s home-grown option keeps getting more capable. fintech.global →
Walmart ends its decade-long holdout on Apple Pay and Google Pay
After years of resistance, Walmart has confirmed it will finally accept Apple Pay and Google Pay in its US stores, with the rollout starting at select locations from 24 August and reaching all Walmart and Sam’s Club tills by year’s end. The retailer had long pushed shoppers towards its own Walmart Pay, even backing a rival payments consortium that folded back in 2016. Apple Pay and Google Pay are now accepted at roughly 85% of US retailers, and Walmart framed the U-turn as giving customers “more choice at checkout.” Worth knowing if you like tap-to-pay wallets to keep spending visible and contactless: one of the last major holdouts has finally come round. Nvidia’s blowout quarter carries the whole market on its back
If you wanted one number to sum up this week on Wall Street, it was $96.2 billion — Nvidia’s second-quarter revenue, more than double what it made a year earlier and comfortably ahead of the $92.2 billion analysts expected. Data-centre sales, the chips powering the world’s AI build-out, came in at $89 billion. Chief executive Jensen Huang put it simply: “compute is revenue, and demand is accelerating.” Shares wobbled briefly on next-quarter guidance that undershot the most bullish forecasts, then reversed to close up more than 5%, dragging the Nasdaq and S&P 500 to fresh record highs alongside strong results from Salesforce and CrowdStrike. It is a reminder of how concentrated this rally has become: one company’s earnings call can move trillions in market value across an entire index. Worth watching: this kind of dependence cuts both ways — spectacular on a beat, brutal on a miss. For now, the AI trade still has fuel, but it is increasingly a bet on a handful of names carrying everyone else. Fortune →  Eastern Herald →
A new Fed chair’s first big test, with the Gulf watching oil
Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May after Jerome Powell’s term ended, gave his first Jackson Hole address today under real pressure. Core inflation is running at 3.3%, well above the Fed’s 2% target, and economists want him to state plainly whether rates might have to rise rather than fall if price pressures do not ease — a message some traders are not ready for after months of rate-cut hope. The backdrop matters too: bond-market volatility and questions over the Treasury’s influence on the Fed have made central-bank independence a live worry for investors, not just a textbook phrase. Meanwhile, easing Iran-Oman talks over the Strait of Hormuz pushed oil to a one-month low near $86 a barrel — a small mercy for inflation-watchers, though a Gulf energy producer like Bahrain feels lower crude prices in its own fiscal arithmetic even as regional tensions ease. Two different pressures, one meeting point: how confidently the world’s most important central bank can steer policy while everyone questions who is really driving. Innovation in investment — tokenised funds go mainstream
Franklin Templeton announced on 20 August that it has secured a first-of-its-kind SEC no-action letter allowing its tokenised BENJI money-market fund — a $2.6bn on-chain product — to sit inside its conventional ETFs and mutual funds as a cash or collateral position. Digital-assets head Sandy Kaul called it “the first time the SEC has said a digitally native product can be used in traditional financial products.” With Franklin overseeing more than $872bn across 130-plus funds, this is worth watching as one of the more concrete steps yet towards blending blockchain-based assets into the everyday fund wrapper millions of ordinary investors already hold, with implementation possibly starting as early as the fourth quarter. Markets brace for a Fed chair who won’t say much
As global investors squared up for Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh’s first Jackson Hole address today, the mood was less “will rates fall” and more “will they rise”. US core inflation has sat above the Fed’s 2% target for 65 consecutive months, running at 3.3% annually in July, and three regional Fed presidents dissented in favour of a hike at July’s meeting — the most hawkish split since 2016. CME’s FedWatch tool put the odds of a September rate rise at around 40%, down from 55% a month earlier, but still a live possibility markets have not fully priced out. Complicating matters, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been buying up long-dated bonds in an attempt to suppress yields that hit their highest level since 2007, a move investor Stanley Druckenmiller called “a subsidy to procrastination” in a Wall Street Journal op-ed. Analysts at Standard Chartered said Warsh needed to “bluntly state” that the Fed will raise rates if inflation does not ease. Meanwhile gold has had its best month since 1999 and bitcoin is up more than 25% in August, as investors hedge against policy uncertainty in either direction. (see Flagged) Euronews →  CNBC →
Deutsche Bank bets big on cutting core systems from 15 to 2
Deutsche Bank’s Private Bank announced this week that it has chosen Vault Core, a cloud-native core banking platform built by UK fintech Thought Machine, as the first of two new systems set to eventually replace a patchwork of 15 legacy platforms by 2028. GFT Technologies has been appointed as integration partner, with testing due by the end of 2026 and phased migration of Personal Banking and Wealth Management products in Germany beginning the following year; old and new systems will run in parallel throughout to protect stability for customers. The bank expects to spend around €600 million on IT, operations and AI through 2028, and forecasts €300 million in annual run-rate savings once complete — illustrating a wider pattern of banks treating decades-old core infrastructure, not just customer-facing apps, as the real bottleneck to innovation. Chief Operating Officer Yiping Li said the move would “create a more scalable foundation for future growth,” while Thought Machine chief executive Paul Taylor called it a precedent for how major international banks modernise. It is a reminder that digital banking increasingly means rebuilding the plumbing, not just the app. (see Flagged) Deutsche Bank →
✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
Somewhere in a Yale University vault sits the world’s oldest working financial instrument: a Dutch perpetual bond, issued on 15 May 1648 by a water authority to fund flood defences along the River Lek. It promised interest “forever” — and, remarkably, it still delivers. Yale acquired it in 2003 and periodically sends a curator to the Netherlands to collect the payout in person; in 2015, a dozen years’ worth of interest came to just €136.20. Nearly 378 years on, not a single payment has been missed. Proof that a well-structured promise — and patience — can quietly outlast empires. Yale News →
