Moneybox's manifesto: turning 7 million cash savers into investors
UK investing app Moneybox unveiled a six-point "retail investing manifesto" in London on 21 October, aiming to move seven million cash-only savers into stocks-and-shares investing by 2035. Proposals include expanding the Lifetime ISA, building low-cost "home-bias" index funds modelled on Sweden's AP7 Såfa fund, extending workplace auto-enrolment beyond pensions into saving and investing, and appointing a government champion to lead a ten-year national strategy. Moneybox's own research found 83% of UK adults feel confident about saving, versus just 40% about investing — the gap this manifesto targets. The company estimates seven million UK adults hold £10,000 or more sitting in cash. Educational, not advice.

Acorns buys peer-learning app Frich to reach young investors earlier
US micro-investing app Acorns announced on 19 October it has acquired Frich, a financial-education community of 1.6 million members, mostly Gen Z. Frich lets members benchmark spending and saving habits against peers and learn through creator-led videos rather than formal lessons. Financial terms were undisclosed. Frich's founders, Katrin Kaurov and Aleksandra Medina, have joined Acorns to co-lead education and community, and Frich's members are migrating onto the Acorns platform. Acorns CEO Noah Kerner said investing feels intimidating, and doing it alongside others helps people move past that — extending Acorns' strategy of meeting would-be investors through relatable, social content before they place a trade.

Sister-founded Prismm tackles "who gets the money" after a death (see Flagged)
Prismm, founded by sisters Martha Underwood and Shella Sylla, pitched at TechCrunch Disrupt's Startup Battlefield this month with tools letting banks build beneficiary and estate planning into everyday accounts. Prismm Designate lets customers name beneficiaries in advance; Prismm Anchor gives families a secure place to track accounts, documents and property across institutions, so transfers can complete remotely without hunting for paperwork. Underwood, a former bank employee, said grieving families — often women, who typically handle this administrative burden — are left managing complex paperwork alone, and banks lose billions in deposits yearly when heirs move inherited money elsewhere.

Netflix's profit jumps 44% — but guidance, not growth, rattles investors
Netflix's Q3 2026 results, released 20 October, showed net income up 44% year-on-year to $3.66bn on revenue of $12.87bn, up 11.8%. Operating margin expanded to 34.6% from 28.2% a year earlier, and diluted EPS of $0.87 beat the $0.82 Wall Street expected, per Reuters-sourced reporting. The company credited subscriber growth, recent price rises and advertising gains. The complication was guidance: Netflix trimmed its full-year 2026 advertising-revenue outlook to roughly $2.7bn from an earlier $3bn target, citing a slower rollout of some ad-product features, and guided Q4 net income to $2.79bn and EPS of $0.67 — below the $0.73 analysts had modelled. For the full year, Netflix still expects revenue near $51.1bn, up 13%, with a 31.5% operating margin. The quarter illustrates a pattern repeating across this earnings season: solid results, overshadowed by cautious guidance.

Tesla beats on earnings, then slips as a capex cut raises AI questions
A day after Netflix's mixed reception, Tesla reported Q3 2026 results on 21 October — and investors responded in a strikingly similar way. Adjusted EPS of $0.48 beat the $0.45 consensus, and total gross margin improved to 19.6% from 18.0% a year earlier. Deliveries of 486,532 vehicles topped the 461,974 expected, even though down 2.1% year-on-year. GAAP net income fell 43% to $787m as operating expenses rose on AI and Optimus robotics spending. The figure that moved the stock was capital expenditure: Tesla lowered its 2026 capex guidance to roughly $20bn, from the "more than $25bn" it had previously signalled, without specifying which projects would see reduced spending. Shares, which had briefly risen about 2% on the headline beat, reversed to close down roughly 3.3% in after-hours trading as investors weighed what the pullback implied for Tesla's robotaxi and AI ambitions.

One to be careful of
Netflix (NFLX)
Netflix is worth understanding this week for its headwinds, not its headline profit. Q3 2026 net income rose 44% to $3.66bn and EPS beat estimates, yet the stock fell to a 52-week low (~$65, -25% year to date) after management cut its full-year advertising-revenue forecast to about $2.7bn from $3bn, delayed ad-tier expansion to 15 new markets until 2027, announced cuts of roughly 6% of staff (~1,000 jobs), and gave Q4 guidance below Wall Street's expectations. Innovation in investment
"Uncapped" sector ETFs
State Street this week launched four new S&P 500 sector SPDR ETFs (tech, consumer discretionary, consumer staples, energy) that exploit a regulatory exception to give megacap stocks their full market weight, unlike traditional sector ETFs, which must cap any single holding at 25%. The result: Nvidia is ~21% of the new tech fund versus ~15.5% in State Street's existing XLK, and Amazon is ~43% of the new consumer-discretionary fund versus ~23% in the old one. It's a notable structural innovation worth watching because it lets investors choose between "diversified" and "market-reality" sector exposure — though it also concentrates risk in a handful of giant companies.

Markets wobble into the Fed as AI-spending doubts spread
US stocks posted their worst week since June in the week to 16 October, with the pullback tracing to doubts about AI-related capital spending rather than to the Federal Reserve. The Nasdaq Composite fell 4.48% and the S&P 500 dropped 1.95%, even as the 10-year Treasury yield eased to 5.09% — normally a tailwind for growth stocks. The iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) lost 4.14%, with AMD, Intel, Micron and Broadcom all down more than 4%, and the VIX volatility index jumped 22.5% to 20.16. ING strategists said Brent crude, up to roughly $110 on Middle East supply risk, was "pricing in a significant risk premium." RBC Capital Markets responded by upgrading healthcare to overweight and cutting technology to market weight, calling healthcare "a natural destination to look to when dialling down Tech exposure." Attention this week turned to earnings from Meta, Tesla and Netflix for evidence AI spending is paying off, while the Fed entered its pre-meeting quiet period ahead of the 27–28 October rate decision. These are analyst views and market data as reported, not investment advice.

JPMorgan draws a line on AI agents in banking
JPMorgan Chase is restricting external AI agents — such as Meta's "Muse," launched in September — from directly accessing or acting on customer bank accounts via shared login credentials, putting a major bank at the centre of the debate over AI and financial security. On JPMorgan's Q3 earnings call (13 October), CFO Jeremy Barnum said credential-sharing with AI agents "is not secure," warning that customers who hand credentials to an agent could bear responsibility if it misuses data or makes unauthorised transactions. CEO Jamie Dimon separately said, "You don't want rogue agents." The bank said it instead supports customer-authorised data sharing through regulated open-banking channels, calling customer protection its "true north." The OECD.AI Incidents Monitor logged the move as an AI-risk event, with coverage noting Charles Schwab and Citi examining similar steps. The episode illustrates a wider bank strategy shift: embracing AI internally while tightening control over how external AI products can touch customer accounts. Educational framing only — not advice to use or avoid any AI banking tool.

A $100 bill costs less than 12 cents to print
Here's one to make you look at your wallet differently: according to the Federal Reserve, it costs just 4.1 cents to print a $1 bill — and only 11.3 cents to print a $100 bill. The crispest note in your purse, worth a hundred real dollars, costs less than a coffee stirrer to produce. The markup isn't inflation; it's simply printing. Paper, ink and a little labour are the only "cost of goods" behind the most valuable note in circulation. Money doesn't need to cost money to be worth a lot.
