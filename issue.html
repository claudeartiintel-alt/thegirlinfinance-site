Abu Dhabi's Mal raises the Gulf's biggest-ever fintech seed round
Mal, a new Abu Dhabi-based finance app built around Islamic finance principles, has raised $230 million in seed funding — reportedly the largest seed round any fintech has publicly raised in the Middle East and Africa. The app wants to fold everyday money tasks (sending and receiving funds, paying bills, financing, investing) into one place rather than several separate providers, and already holds in-principle approval from the UAE's central bank. It has opened a global waitlist with joining bonuses for early sign-ups. Worth knowing: "do it all in one app" banking is becoming a serious contender in the Gulf — a trend worth watching even if switching apps isn't on your radar yet.

The financial coach you might already have through work
Workplace financial-wellness platform BrightPlan has closed a Series C round led by ABS Capital Partners, with more investors expected within 90 days. The company says it now reaches over 10 million employees across 50+ countries, pairing AI guidance with human, in-country advisers on budgeting, saving and investing under local rules, and reports 230% revenue growth over three years (company-stated figures). It's a sign more employers are adding financial coaching alongside health insurance and retirement matching. Worth knowing: if your employer offers a benefit like this, it's usually free to use — a low-pressure way to get a second opinion on a money question before turning to the internet at midnight.

Bahrain's Beyon Money plugs straight into crypto
Beyon Money Business and crypto exchange BitOasis Bahrain have switched on a direct link between their platforms, letting eligible Beyon Money wallet users fund a BitOasis account without a separate card payment or bank transfer first. The integration runs inside the Central Bank of Bahrain's regulatory sandbox-turned-framework, with both firms framing it as proof that licensed, closely supervised fintechs and digital-asset platforms can connect safely. It's a small technical update rather than a flashy new app, but a useful local signal: Bahrain's regulators are actively letting conventional digital wallets and crypto platforms talk to each other, rather than keeping them in separate lanes.

What happened: oil spikes as the US–Iran tanker war escalates
Markets spent this week reacting to a sharp escalation in the months-long US–Iran conflict. On 5–6 September, US forces struck three Iranian oil tankers, and Tehran responded with missiles aimed at American warships plus strikes on further vessels — a tit-for-tat pattern US Central Command has described as an economic-cost exchange over shipping. Iran has kept pressure on the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil normally flows, though tracking firm Kpler says actual tanker traffic through it has fallen sharply during the conflict. The result: Brent crude, trading near $70/barrel before the war began in March, surged to about $105/barrel by 10 September — its highest since May — while US diesel hit a record average near $5.85/gallon. US equities fell for a fourth consecutive session, and Treasury yields climbed to fresh 52-week highs as investors priced in both the supply risk and its inflationary knock-on effects.

What it means: oil-driven inflation is pushing the Fed toward a possible hike
For savers and borrowers, the mechanism matters more than the headline. Higher oil feeds directly into wholesale prices: August's Producer Price Index rose 0.4% on the month, with annual wholesale inflation up 5.4% — above forecasts — largely on energy costs. Combined with a surprisingly strong August jobs report, traders have pushed the probability of a Fed rate increase at the 16–17 September meeting to roughly 60–70% on different trackers, a sharp jump from under 45% a month ago. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh signalled at Jackson Hole in August that price stability, not employment softness, is now the Fed's "predominant focus". Educationally: a rate hike (rather than the cuts markets expected earlier this year) would raise borrowing costs on mortgages, credit cards and loans, while potentially supporting the dollar — not something to act on, just the chain of cause and effect worth understanding ahead of next week. One to be careful of — Cooper Companies
Cooper Companies is a reminder that beating earnings isn't the whole story. On 9 September the eyecare and fertility group beat EPS estimates ($1.15 vs $1.12 consensus), yet shares sank over 15% because revenue grew just 1%, full-year guidance implied only 2–3% organic growth, and a strategic review ended with management keeping its fertility unit rather than selling it as investors had hoped. CEO Al White also flagged US inventory reductions at CooperVision weighing on Q4. Not a signal to act on — simply a case worth understanding in how guidance and strategic clarity can matter more than the headline number.

Innovation in investment — prediction-market ETFs
A genuinely new corner of investing opened on 9 September when Tema ETFs launched DICE on Cboe — the first ETF built around prediction-markets infrastructure. It holds stakes in Kalshi and Polymarket alongside Robinhood, Coinbase, Interactive Brokers, Circle and Intercontinental Exchange, charging a 0.75% expense ratio. Management cites forecasts of prediction-market volume growing nearly 20x to $1 trillion by 2030. Worth understanding as one of the clearest signs yet that "betting on outcomes" platforms are being packaged into mainstream, regulated fund wrappers.

How banks are evolving: 21 global banks move on a joint stablecoin
Reporting confirmed this week that 21 major banks — including Bank of America, Citi, Goldman Sachs, UBS, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Wells Fargo and Santander — are forming a joint venture to issue a US-dollar stablecoin, targeting a first-half-2027 launch. The move follows the GENIUS Act, US legislation effective January 2027 that requires 1:1 reserve backing for stablecoins and restricts who may issue them. Analysts frame this as a "dual strategy": tokenised deposits to defend existing customer relationships, alongside a public, blockchain-based stablecoin to compete for new digital-asset flows — even as JPMorgan builds its own proprietary systems rather than joining the consortium. A parallel euro-denominated effort, Qivalis, is being built by a separate European bank consortium for the second half of 2026. Together, these signal banks moving from cautiously watching crypto infrastructure to directly building and owning it.

✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
The 1974 law that finally let women have their own credit card
Until 1974, a woman in the US could have a steady job and her own income and still be turned down for a credit card in her own name — unless a man (husband, father, even a brother) was willing to co-sign. Banks routinely asked female applicants about marital status before even looking at their income. It took the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 to make it illegal for lenders to judge an applicant's creditworthiness by gender rather than their actual finances. So next time you tap your own card with your own name on it: that's a hard-won 1970s victory, not a given.
