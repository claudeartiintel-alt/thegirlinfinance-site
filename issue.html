🌱 Personal finance: tools, ideas & innovation
Tool — a private banker’s-eye view of your own money
A new London app called Quartz launched this week, promising ordinary savers the kind of joined-up financial picture usually reserved for private-wealth clients. Built by a former Revolut expansion lead and an ex-N26 product lead, Quartz pulls your pensions, ISAs, savings and investment accounts into one dashboard, then layers on an AI assistant named “Charlie” that surfaces patterns and answers questions about your own numbers. The team raised £2.75 million in pre-seed funding led by VC firm Daphni, and says members already have more than £10 million in assets being tracked during its beta. It is registered with the UK’s Financial Conduct Authority as an account-information provider — worth checking for, generally, before you link any app to your bank.
Source: tech.eu
Fintech innovation — buy-now-pay-later apps are quietly becoming full bank accounts
Saudi Arabia’s Tabby, best known as a buy-now-pay-later app, raised $233 million this cycle and nearly doubled its valuation to $6.5 billion — a sign of how fast Gulf fintech is maturing beyond simple instalment plans. More relevant for everyday users: Tabby picked up new consumer and SME finance licences in Saudi Arabia and a Stored Value Facilities licence in the UAE, where it is launching “Tabby Cash”, pitched as a fee-free alternative to a standard debit account. It also acquired Tweeq, a licensed digital wallet. Together, these moves show a BNPL app quietly becoming a full financial account — useful to know if you already use one, since the product you signed up for may soon do far more than let you split a payment.
Source: Wamda
Money idea — starting small and early counts, even without a deposit saved
A new U.S. Bank survey published this week offers a useful mirror for anyone questioning their own money timeline. Gen Z respondents said they typically start building wealth at 19, versus 25 for millennials and 32 for baby boomers — yet 56% feel they are doing everything “right” and still are not where they hoped to be. The idea worth borrowing: 62% of Gen Z and 61% of millennials now see stock market investing as a more realistic path to long-term wealth than saving for a house, even though most still call traditional investing the soundest long-term strategy. The takeaway is not to abandon bigger goals — it is that starting small, early, and without a deposit already saved is a legitimate financial move in its own right, not a consolation prize.
Source: Business Wire / U.S. Bank

Why the Fed raised rates instead of cutting them
Most of 2026 had investors betting the Federal Reserve’s next move would be a rate cut. Instead, on 16 September the Fed’s policy committee voted unanimously to raise its benchmark rate a quarter-point, to a range of 3.75–4% — the first increase since 2023. The benchmark rate is what banks charge each other overnight; when it rises, everything from credit-card APRs to mortgage rates tends to follow. The Fed’s reasoning: a stronger-than-expected inflation report (core prices rose 0.3% in a single month) showed price pressures had not cooled as hoped, even as the Fed said the economy is “expanding at a solid pace” with steady hiring. The majority of Fed officials now pencil in at least one more hike this year. Stocks initially rose on the news, then reversed sharply, with the S&P 500 falling roughly 1% to a six-week low. For newcomers: a hike generally means borrowing gets pricier and savings accounts pay a little more — a trade-off, not simply good or bad news. This is educational context, not investing advice.
Sources: Federal Reserve · Charles Schwab
The bond market’s own verdict: yields last seen in 2007
Beyond the Fed’s own move, the more dramatic story this week played out in the bond market. The 10-year US Treasury yield — essentially the interest rate the government pays to borrow for a decade, and a benchmark mortgage rates track closely — climbed to about 5.11%, its highest since 2007, the year before the global financial crisis. The 30-year and even the 2-year yields hit multi-year highs too. Weak demand at recent government debt auctions, heavy federal borrowing, and hawkish comments from a senior Fed official all fed the move. Rising yields matter for everyday finances because they pull other borrowing costs up with them and make already-elevated stock valuations look less attractive by comparison — largely why the S&P 500, Dow and Nasdaq fell for a third straight session, with fewer than a third of S&P 500 stocks trading above their 50-day average. None of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
Sources: TheStreet · BNN Bloomberg

The company to follow
Meta Platforms
Meta is worth watching after its stock climbed roughly 25% in September — its strongest month in over two years — following the launch of “Muse”, a personal AI assistant that tries to execute tasks such as booking reservations and managing email through connected apps. It became the number-one free app in the US App Store within a week, overtaking ChatGPT. The story got more interesting when Amazon and Google reportedly moved to restrict Muse’s access to their own platforms — a sign of how seriously rivals are taking it. For anyone curious about how “AI agents” might change everyday tasks rather than just chat, this is one worth watching play out.
Sources: CNBC · Yahoo Finance UK
One to be careful of
Oracle
Oracle is a useful reminder that “AI boom” stocks carry real execution risk. This week Oracle invoked force majeure on Project Jupiter, its $165 billion flagship AI data centre in New Mexico (part of the Stargate initiative with OpenAI and SoftBank), after a key energy permit was denied and financing debt tied to the project began trading in distressed territory. Shares fell roughly 4–5% on the news. Oracle says its near-term earnings outlook is unaffected, but the episode shows how dependent AI-infrastructure buildouts are on permits, power supply and financing conditions holding together — worth understanding if you hold AI-adjacent names or funds. This is not a signal to act on either way.
Sources: CNBC · Yahoo Finance
Innovation in investment
The REX AI Chipmaking ETF (CHIP)
A new thematic fund launched on Nasdaq this week that is worth understanding as an example of how “picks and shovels” investing works. The REX AI Chipmaking ETF (ticker CHIP), launched 22 September, does not invest in chip designers like Nvidia — instead it holds around 55 companies that make the equipment behind AI chips: wafer-fabrication machinery, advanced packaging and precision testing, tracking the VettaFi AI Chipmaking Index. It charges a 0.65% expense ratio. It is a good real-world example of investing in a whole layer of an industry rather than a single company — a concept worth understanding even if this particular fund is not for you, since niche thematic funds carry concentration risk alongside their upside.
Sources: ETF Database · Yahoo Finance
A new-CEO adventure that will make a difference
Kevin Rayment, Curtiss-Wright
Aerospace-and-defence supplier Curtiss-Wright announced this week that Kevin Rayment, currently its COO and a 35-year industry veteran, will become President and CEO from 1 January 2027, succeeding Lynn Bamford, who moves to Executive Chair. It is a textbook orderly succession story worth understanding: it is happening from a position of strength, not crisis — Bamford’s six years included record sales, profitability and backlog, and $1.7 billion returned to shareholders — with the board framing it as continuity of strategy rather than a rescue mission. Planned CEO transitions like this are generally read very differently by markets than sudden departures, which is itself a useful thing to understand as an investor.
Source: Yahoo Finance
🔮 Investment: the bigger picture
Where forecasters think markets go from here
Wall Street’s mood turned more cautious this week after the Fed’s 16 September rate rise. The same day, veteran strategist Ed Yardeni cut his 2026 S&P 500 target by 500 points, from 8,400 to 7,900, pointing to the 10-year Treasury yield nearing 5%, mortgage rates approaching 7%, oil above $100 a barrel amid Iran-related tensions, and uncertainty ahead of November’s midterm elections — though he still projects 8,400 by mid-2027 and 10,000 by the end of the decade. By contrast, Goldman Sachs Asset Management’s outlook stayed “overweight equities”, citing strong earnings (consensus sees 31% EPS growth in the US this year) and GDP growth re-accelerating to 2.1% in 2027 as inflation nears the Fed’s 2% target — while flagging that uncertainty about the Fed’s new leadership “may raise rate volatility in the near term”. These are analysts’ reported views, not guarantees; forecasts like these are routinely revised as new data arrives.
Sources: The Motley Fool · Goldman Sachs Asset Management
How banks are evolving: teaching AI to use decades-old systems
A story this week shows banks trying to leapfrog their oldest technology rather than replace it. On 22 September, industry outlet PYMNTS reported that OpenAI’s newest model can operate a bank’s legacy computer systems autonomously — reading screens, filling in forms and clicking between programmes much like a human employee — instead of requiring banks to rewrite the decades-old code that still runs core operations. The scale of the problem is striking: an estimated 95% of US ATM transactions still run on code dating to 1959, and 45 of the world’s 50 largest banks rely on mainframes. Replacing a core system the traditional way is slow and costly — Commonwealth Bank of Australia’s overhaul reportedly took five years and cost more than A$1 billion. Goldman Sachs is testing the new approach, having spent six months working with Anthropic engineers to build AI agents for trade accounting and client onboarding. The technology is still immature, so this reads as an early-stage shift rather than a finished transformation.
Source: PYMNTS
✨ Girl in Finance Fun Fact Friday
Here is one that might make you gasp: until 1974, a woman in America — whatever her income or credit history — usually could not get a credit card in her own name. Banks required a male co-signer: a husband, father, brother, even a family friend, just to vouch for her. It did not matter if she paid every bill on time; the rules simply were not about her financial ability. That changed only when Congress passed the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in 1974, banning credit discrimination based on sex or marital status. Fifty-odd years later, that old “no” is why every card in your wallet today has your own name on it.
Source: US National Archives
